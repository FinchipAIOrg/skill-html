02 · Before / After

Three shifts: the basis for an endpoint, the source of a sample size, the gap behind a recruitment plan

The same design move, taken from "this looks reasonable" to "we know where it will be challenged".

01

Endpoints: from "this looks reasonable" to "we know where it will be challenged"

Before

The candidate endpoints are listed and the primary is chosen on instinct and precedent. An unvalidated surrogate looks like a good fit, and nobody can say whether it can hold the primary endpoint position with regulators.

After

Every candidate is scored across five weighted dimensions (clinical relevance, measurability, regulatory acceptance, sensitivity to change, patient burden) and classified as PRIMARY, KEY-SECONDARY or EXPLORATORY. An unvalidated surrogate is flagged and demoted, and cannot become the primary endpoint.

From "this endpoint looks reasonable" to "here is where it will be challenged, and why".

02

Sample size: from "an effect size we can reach" to "an effect size we can trace"

Before

Sample size is often worked backwards: see how many patients can be enrolled, then choose the effect size that makes that number work. Dropout is written into the assumptions but never enters the calculation, so n comes out too small.

After

A closed-form solution per design: Cohen's d for means, a normal approximation for proportions, Schoenfeld event counts for survival. The sample size is inflated by the expected dropout rate, and the printed assumption block asks you to trace the effect size back to a published or anchor source.

From "the number adds up" to "the number can be traced".

03

Feasibility: from "we should be able to enrol" to "the gap is on this line"

Before

The enrolment plan comes from optimistic site estimates. A dozen sites, a dozen months, a couple of hundred patients: nobody separated "is the eligible population big enough" from "how many patients can each site enrol per month".

After

Feasibility is scored 0 to 100 across five dimensions (recruitment, endpoint readiness, statistical power, operational complexity, budget fit). Anything below threshold becomes a named blocker and decides the verdict directly: GO, GO-WITH-CONDITIONS, REDESIGN or NO-GO.

From "we should be able to enrol" to "the gap is on this line, and this person has to sign".

03 · Pain point and use case

What stalls a protocol is rarely the science. It is three numbers nobody actually ran.

They live in different places, and the work of joining them up stays with people.

Number 01The endpoint has to hold the primary positionThe evidence is scattered across literature, precedent and regulatory tables, and the choice is made on instinct.
Number 02The sample size has to hold the powerIt lives on a biostatistician's schedule. Dropout is written into the assumptions but never enters the calculation.
Number 03Recruitment has to land inside time and budgetIt comes from optimistic site estimates, and nobody separated eligible population from patients per site per month.

The problem is not a lack of tools. Statistics software computes sample sizes, regulatory guidance documents endpoint precedent, and a spreadsheet assembles a feasibility table. The problem is that each tool answers one segment of the question.

Worse, what comes out the other end looks a lot like a conclusion. A standard-looking sample size, a plausible enrolment window, a workable site count: none of them is marked as an assumption when it is written into the protocol.

These are the numbers that cause trouble at the phase gate. The committee rarely asks whether you ran the calculation. It asks where the effect size came from, why dropout was set at 15%, and who verified the eligible population. When nobody can answer, the protocol is sent back for what looks like a design flaw. Underneath, it is that nobody can say where the basis sits.

One risk is quieter still: an unvalidated surrogate treated as the primary endpoint. It looks sensitive, cheap and easy to measure early on, right up to the point where regulators say it cannot hold the primary position. By then, the whole design has to be redone.

04 · Transition

What is missing is not a fourth tool. It is a layer that keeps the basis attached.

Every estimate should arrive together with its assumptions, its source and its limits. Every decision should know whose signature makes it stand.

That is what this Skill does. It adds no authority to the judgement. It leaves a trace of it.

07 · Core advantages

Five differences that reach the user

01

Deterministic output you can recompute and audit

No model calls, no network, standard library only. The same input always returns the same result, and anyone can take the parameters and recompute a conclusion line by line. Where a protocol has to pass a gate, reproducibility matters more than looking clever.

02

Every output carries its limits, not just a conclusion

All outputs carry an ESTIMATE banner stating that this is a first-pass estimate and that the final sample size must be confirmed by a biostatistician using simulation, an adaptive design or an exact method. It deliberately avoids the tone of a conclusion.

03

The decision stays with named people

A verdict is not an authorization. It is a recommendation with a blocker list, followed by the roles that must sign: Principal Investigator, Medical Monitor, Biostatistician, Regulatory Owner, Study Director, depending on the verdict tier. Onboarding replaces those roles with real names and prints them into the output.

04

An unvalidated surrogate cannot take the primary position

A surrogate endpoint with no validation evidence is flagged and demoted, and cannot become the primary endpoint. That rule moves one of the most expensive kinds of late rework back into the design stage.

05

The development area actually changes the result

drug, device, biologic, diagnostic and digital-therapeutic each adjust the per-patient cost benchmark and the recruitment difficulty coefficient. This is not a relabelled dropdown: budget fit and recruitment feasibility move with it.

08 · Why it matters

Giving a design decision its first paper trail

What this Skill reduces is not a few calculations. It is the same thing that keeps happening in clinical design: a number is produced, written into a protocol, questioned in review, and then nobody can say where it came from.

When every estimate arrives with its assumptions, its source and its limits, and when every verdict knows whose signature makes it stand, the design process moves from "that is what we decided at the time" to "here is the basis, recompute it". The first set of parameters can carry into the next revision, the first blocker can be tracked to closure, and the first named owner can keep the same thread.

For a clinical team, what is saved is not time. It is rework.
